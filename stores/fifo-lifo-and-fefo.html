1Chapter 1: Introduction to Inventory Management

1.1Meaning of Inventory

Inventory consists of goods and materials that a business:

  • Holds for sale to customers
  • Uses in the production of goods
  • Consumes while providing services
  • Keeps as raw materials, work in progress, or finished products

For a retailer, inventory may include clothing, food, electronics, or household goods. For a manufacturer, inventory may include raw materials, partially completed products, and finished goods. For a hospital or pharmacy, inventory may include medicines, medical supplies, and laboratory materials.

Inventory is usually classified into three major categories:

Raw Materials

Raw materials are inputs that have not yet entered the production process. Examples include:

  • Steel used to manufacture automobiles
  • Flour used in a bakery
  • Fabric used by a clothing manufacturer
  • Active pharmaceutical ingredients used to produce medicine

Work in Progress

Work-in-progress inventory consists of goods that have entered production but are not yet complete.

For example, an automobile partly assembled on a factory floor is work in progress.

Finished Goods

Finished goods are completed products that are ready for sale or distribution.

Examples include:

  • Packaged food in a supermarket warehouse
  • Completed laptops awaiting shipment
  • Bottled medicine in a pharmaceutical distribution center

1.2Inventory Management

Inventory management is the process of planning, purchasing, receiving, storing, tracking, protecting, and issuing inventory.

Effective inventory management attempts to balance two risks:

  1. Holding too much inventory
  2. Holding too little inventory

Excessive inventory can result in:

  • Storage costs
  • Insurance costs
  • Damage
  • Theft
  • Obsolescence
  • Expired products
  • Cash being tied up in stock

Insufficient inventory can result in:

  • Lost sales
  • Production interruptions
  • Customer dissatisfaction
  • Emergency purchasing
  • Higher transportation costs
  • Damage to the business’s reputation

Inventory methods such as FIFO, LIFO, and FEFO help organizations decide which units should be issued or sold and how inventory costs should be measured.

1.3Inventory Flow and Cost Flow

There are two related but different ideas:

Physical Flow

Physical flow refers to the actual movement of goods through a warehouse or production facility.

For example, older food may physically be sold before newer food.

Cost Flow

Cost flow refers to the accounting assumption used to determine which inventory costs are assigned to:

  • Cost of goods sold
  • Ending inventory

The accounting cost flow does not always match the physical movement of goods.

For example, a business may physically sell its oldest products first but use a weighted-average cost method for financial reporting.

This distinction is especially important for LIFO. Under LIFO, the latest costs are assigned to cost of goods sold, even though the business may physically sell older products first.

2Chapter 2: Why Inventory Valuation Matters

2.1Inventory as an Asset

Inventory is normally reported as a current asset on the statement of financial position, also called the balance sheet.

The basic inventory relationship is:

Rearranged:

Inventory valuation determines the amount reported for both ending inventory and cost of goods sold.

2.2Effect on Profit

The basic gross-profit formula is:

If cost of goods sold increases, gross profit decreases.

If cost of goods sold decreases, gross profit increases.

Therefore, the selected inventory method can affect:

  • Gross profit
  • Operating profit
  • Income before tax
  • Net income
  • Earnings per share
  • Taxable income
  • Inventory reported on the balance sheet

2.3Effect on Taxes

Inventory methods can affect taxes because taxable income is often influenced by reported cost of goods sold.

When prices are rising:

  • LIFO generally produces higher cost of goods sold.
  • Higher cost of goods sold generally produces lower taxable income.
  • Lower taxable income may result in lower current income taxes.

When prices are falling, the effect may be reversed.

Tax rules are jurisdiction-specific. A business must follow the tax laws applicable to its country and should obtain professional tax advice before selecting an inventory method for tax reporting.

2.4Effect on Decision-Making

Inventory valuation affects business decisions such as:

  • Product pricing
  • Purchasing
  • Budgeting
  • Performance measurement
  • Profit distribution
  • Loan applications
  • Investment analysis
  • Warehouse planning

If inventory is overstated, assets and profit may appear higher than they actually are. If inventory is understated, reported profit and assets may appear lower.

Reliable valuation is therefore important for managers, investors, lenders, tax authorities, auditors, and other users of financial statements.

2.5Inventory Valuation and the Lower-of-Cost Principle

Inventory should generally not be reported at an amount greater than the economic benefits expected to be received from selling or using it.

Under many accounting frameworks, inventory is measured at the lower of:

  • Cost
  • Net realizable value

Net realizable value is generally the estimated selling price less the estimated costs of completion and sale.

For example, assume inventory originally cost 10,000 dollars. Because of damage, it can now be sold for 8,500 dollars after selling costs. If the applicable accounting rules require a write-down, the inventory may need to be reported at 8,500 dollars rather than 10,000 dollars.

3Chapter 3: Understanding FIFO, LIFO, and FEFO

3.1FIFO

FIFO
FIFO meansFirst-In, First-Out

The FIFO assumption is that the earliest units purchased or produced are assigned to cost of goods sold first.

The units remaining in ending inventory are generally the most recently purchased or produced units.

FIFO often resembles the physical movement of goods, particularly for products that can deteriorate or become obsolete.

3.2LIFO

LIFO
LIFO meansLast-In, First-Out

The LIFO assumption is that the latest units purchased or produced are assigned to cost of goods sold first.

The units remaining in ending inventory generally reflect older costs.

LIFO is an accounting cost-flow method. It does not require the physical movement of goods to follow a last-in, first-out pattern.

3.3FEFO

FEFO
FEFO meansFirst-Expired, First-Out

Under FEFO, the products with the earliest expiration dates are issued or sold first, regardless of when they were received.

FEFO is primarily an operational inventory-rotation method rather than a general financial-accounting valuation method.

For example, a medicine received in March may expire in September, while a medicine received in February may expire in December. Under FEFO, the March shipment would be issued first because it expires earlier.

3.4Main Difference Between the Methods

Method Main basis for issuing or assigning units Primary purpose
FIFO Earliest purchase or production date Cost-flow assumption and stock rotation
LIFO Latest purchase or production date Cost-flow assumption
FEFO Earliest expiration date Physical stock rotation and safety

FIFO and LIFO are commonly discussed in financial accounting. FEFO is most important for warehouse operations, product safety, and expiration control.

4Chapter 4: FIFO—First-In, First-Out

4.1Definition

FIFO assumes that the first units purchased are the first units sold or issued.

Under FIFO:

  • Older costs are transferred to cost of goods sold first.
  • Newer costs remain in ending inventory.
  • Ending inventory often approximates current replacement cost when prices are rising.

4.5FIFO Under a Periodic System

A periodic inventory system calculates cost of goods sold at the end of the accounting period.

Using the same data:

  • Total units available: 450
  • Units sold: 300
  • Units remaining: 150
  • Total cost available: 5,500 dollars

Under periodic FIFO, the 300 units sold are assigned the oldest costs:

  • 100 units at 10 dollars
  • 200 units at 12 dollars

Cost of goods sold:

(100×10)+(200×12)=3,400

Ending inventory:

150×14=2,100

In this particular example, periodic and perpetual FIFO produce the same result. That will not always happen. The result can differ when purchases and sales occur at several different dates and quantities.

4.6Advantages of FIFO

FIFO has several advantages:

  • It is intuitive and easy to explain.
  • It often reflects the physical movement of perishable goods.
  • Ending inventory is usually based on recent costs.
  • It is permitted under IFRS and US GAAP.
  • It reduces the risk of leaving old, potentially obsolete stock in storage.
  • It is widely supported by accounting and inventory software.
  • It normally avoids unusual negative or outdated inventory layers.

4.7Disadvantages of FIFO

FIFO also has limitations:

  • During inflation, it may produce lower cost of goods sold.
  • During inflation, it may produce higher reported profit.
  • Higher reported profit can result in higher current taxes.
  • Profit may be overstated because older, cheaper costs are matched with current sales prices.
  • It may not reflect current replacement cost in cost of goods sold.

4.8Real-World Uses of FIFO

FIFO is especially suitable for:

  • Fresh food
  • Dairy products
  • Medicines
  • Cosmetics
  • Batteries
  • Chemicals
  • Fashion products
  • Electronic products that become obsolete
  • General retail merchandise

A warehouse may use FIFO physically even when the accounting system uses a different valuation method.

5Chapter 5: LIFO—Last-In, First-Out

5.1Definition

LIFO assumes that the most recently purchased or produced units are assigned to cost of goods sold first.

Under LIFO:

  • Recent costs are matched with current sales revenue.
  • Older costs remain in ending inventory.
  • During inflation, cost of goods sold is usually higher than under FIFO.
  • During inflation, ending inventory is usually lower than under FIFO.

5.2Basic LIFO Example

Use the same inventory information:

Transaction Units Unit cost Total cost
Beginning inventory 100 10 dollars 1,000 dollars
Purchase 1 200 12 dollars 2,400 dollars
Purchase 2 150 14 dollars 2,100 dollars
Total available 450 — 5,500 dollars

Assume the business sells 300 units.

  1. 1
    Step 1

    Assign the newest units to cost of goods sold

    The most recent 150 units are assigned first:

    150×14=2,100

    The business still needs to assign 150 units. These come from the 200 units purchased at 12 dollars:

    150×12=1,800
  2. 2
    Step 2

    Calculate LIFO cost of goods sold

  3. 3
    Step 3

    Calculate ending inventory

    The remaining inventory consists of:

    • 50 units at 12 dollars
    • 100 units at 10 dollars

    Ending inventory:

    (50×12)+(100×10)=600+1,000=1,600
  4. ✓
    Outcome

    Result

    Item Amount
    Cost of goods sold 3,900 dollars
    Ending inventory 1,600 dollars
    Total inventory cost accounted for 5,500 dollars

  • Purchase: 200 units at 12 dollars
  • Sale: 150 units
  • Purchase: 150 units at 14 dollars
  • Sale: 150 units

First sale

After the first purchase, inventory consists of:

  • 100 units at 10 dollars
  • 200 units at 12 dollars

Under perpetual LIFO, the first sale of 150 units uses the newest available layer:

150×12=1,800

Remaining inventory:

  • 100 units at 10 dollars
  • 50 units at 12 dollars

Second purchase

Add 150 units at 14 dollars.

Inventory consists of:

  • 100 units at 10 dollars
  • 50 units at 12 dollars
  • 150 units at 14 dollars

Second sale

The second sale of 150 units uses the newest layer:

150×14=2,100

Perpetual LIFO results

Total cost of goods sold:

1,800 + 2,100 = 3,900

Ending inventory:

(100×10)+(50×12)=1,000+600=1,600

5.5Periodic LIFO

Under periodic LIFO, all purchases for the period are considered together at the end of the period.

The 300 units sold are assigned the newest costs:

  • 150 units at 14 dollars
  • 150 units at 12 dollars

Cost of goods sold:

(150×14)+(150×12)=2,100+1,800=3,900

Ending inventory:

  • 50 units at 12 dollars
  • 100 units at 10 dollars

Ending inventory value:

(50×12)+(100×10)=1,600

5.6Advantages of LIFO

  • During inflation, it generally matches recent costs with current sales revenue.
  • During inflation, it generally produces higher cost of goods sold.
  • Higher cost of goods sold may reduce taxable income under systems that permit LIFO.
  • It may provide a more current measure of operating margin.
  • It can be useful for commodities with interchangeable units and frequently changing costs.

5.7Disadvantages of LIFO

  • It is prohibited by IFRS.
  • It is permitted under US GAAP only subject to applicable requirements.
  • Ending inventory may contain very old costs.
  • Reported inventory may be understated during inflation.
  • LIFO layers can create complicated records.
  • Liquidating old LIFO layers can produce unusual increases in reported profit.
  • It usually does not represent the physical flow of goods.
  • Comparisons with companies using FIFO may be difficult.
  • It may require additional disclosures and reconciliation.

5.8LIFO Liquidation

A LIFO liquidation occurs when a business sells more units than it replaces, causing older LIFO layers to be released into cost of goods sold.

Suppose a company has:

  • 100 units at 10 dollars
  • 100 units at 12 dollars
  • Current sales require 250 units

If no new inventory is purchased, the business must use the old 10-dollar layer for part of the sale. Because those costs are lower than current replacement costs, cost of goods sold may be unusually low and profit may be unusually high.

LIFO liquidation can therefore make current-period profit appear stronger even though the business has reduced its inventory.

5.9Real-World Uses of LIFO

LIFO may be used in some US businesses dealing with:

  • Oil and gas products
  • Metals
  • Construction materials
  • Industrial commodities
  • Homogeneous goods with changing costs

However, businesses handling food, medicines, and other perishable products normally should not use LIFO as a physical stock-rotation method.

6Chapter 6: FEFO—First-Expired, First-Out

6.1Definition

FEFO means First-Expired, First-Out.

Under FEFO, inventory is issued according to expiration date rather than receipt date or purchase cost.

The product with the earliest expiration date is selected first.

6.2FEFO Example

A pharmacy has the following medicine batches:

Batch Receipt date Expiration date Quantity Unit cost
A January 5 September 30 100 8 dollars
B January 20 July 31 80 9 dollars
C February 10 December 31 120 10 dollars

Although Batch A was received first, Batch B expires first.

Under FEFO, the issue sequence is:

  1. Batch B
  2. Batch A
  3. Batch C

If the pharmacy issues 150 units:

  • 80 units come from Batch B
  • 70 units come from Batch A

The operational issue cost depends on the accounting valuation method selected.

If specific identification is used:

(80×9)+(70×8)=720+560=1,280

If weighted average is used, the issue may be recorded using the applicable average unit cost instead.

6.3FEFO Is Not Normally a Separate Accounting Valuation Method

FEFO answers this question:

Which physical units should be issued first?

Accounting valuation answers this question:

Which costs should be assigned to goods sold and ending inventory?

A company can therefore:

  • Physically issue goods using FEFO
  • Value inventory using FIFO
  • Value inventory using weighted average
  • Use specific identification for traceable batches

This combination is common in pharmaceuticals, food distribution, chemicals, and medical supplies.

6.4Why FEFO Is Important

FEFO helps reduce:

  • Expired inventory
  • Product waste
  • Customer safety risks
  • Product recalls
  • Regulatory violations
  • Emergency disposal costs
  • Losses from obsolete stock

It is especially important when products have different expiration dates.

6.5FEFO and Shelf Life

Shelf life is the period during which a product remains suitable for use or sale.

A warehouse should monitor:

  • Manufacturing date
  • Receipt date
  • Expiration date
  • Lot or batch number
  • Remaining shelf life
  • Storage conditions
  • Required minimum shelf life at shipment

For example, a customer may require that a product have at least six months of remaining shelf life when delivered. The warehouse system must identify whether a batch meets that requirement.

6.6FEFO Advantages

  1. Reduces expiration-related losses.
  2. Supports product safety.
  3. Is appropriate for medicines and food.
  4. Helps organizations comply with regulatory requirements.
  5. Improves customer satisfaction.
  6. Supports recall management.
  7. Makes batch-level stock control more effective.

6.7FEFO Disadvantages

  1. It requires accurate expiration-date records.
  2. It may require advanced warehouse software.
  3. Warehouse staff need training.
  4. Products may need to be rearranged frequently.
  5. The earliest-expiring item may be located behind newer items.
  6. It does not, by itself, determine financial inventory valuation.
  7. Incorrect data entry can cause unsafe or inefficient issuing decisions.

6.8FEFO Versus FIFO

FIFO selects the earliest received goods.

FEFO selects the goods that will expire first.

These methods produce the same result only when the oldest received goods also have the earliest expiration dates.

For example:

Batch Receipt date Expiration date
A January 1 December 31
B February 1 June 30

FIFO would issue Batch A first. FEFO would issue Batch B first.

In this situation, FEFO is safer because Batch B will expire earlier.

7Chapter 7: Perpetual and Periodic Inventory Systems

7.1Perpetual Inventory System

A perpetual system updates inventory records continuously.

Each transaction may update:

  • Quantity on hand
  • Inventory cost
  • Cost of goods sold
  • Inventory layers
  • Batch or lot information

Advantages

  • Provides current inventory information
  • Supports automated reorder points
  • Helps detect shortages
  • Supports detailed management reporting
  • Works well with barcode and RFID systems
  • Is useful for high-value or regulated goods

Disadvantages

  • Costs more to implement
  • Requires reliable technology
  • Depends on accurate transaction recording
  • Still requires physical counts for verification

7.2Periodic Inventory System

A periodic system does not update the accounting inventory balance after every sale.

Instead, the business performs a physical count at the end of the period.

The formula is:

Advantages

  • Simpler to operate
  • Less expensive for small businesses
  • Requires less transaction-level technology
  • Can be appropriate for low-volume operations

Disadvantages

  • Current inventory may not be known during the period
  • Theft and damage may be discovered late
  • Detailed batch tracking is difficult
  • Cost of goods sold is calculated only after counting and valuation
  • Less suitable for medicines, perishable food, and regulated products

7.3Perpetual and Periodic Example

Assume:

  • Beginning inventory: 100 units at 10 dollars
  • Purchase 1: 200 units at 12 dollars
  • Sale 1: 150 units
  • Purchase 2: 150 units at 14 dollars
  • Sale 2: 150 units

Total units sold are 300.

Under perpetual FIFO:

  • First sale: 100 units at 10 dollars and 50 units at 12 dollars
  • Second sale: 150 units at 12 dollars
  • Total cost of goods sold: 3,400 dollars

Under periodic FIFO:

  • Total units sold are assigned using the oldest costs available at the end of the period
  • 100 units at 10 dollars and 200 units at 12 dollars
  • Total cost of goods sold: 3,400 dollars

In other transaction patterns, the results may differ because a perpetual system calculates cost at each sale while a periodic system calculates cost after considering all purchases for the period.

Under rising prices:

  • FIFO generally reports the highest gross profit.
  • LIFO generally reports the lowest gross profit.
  • Weighted average generally falls between FIFO and LIFO.

8.4Effect on the Income Statement

The inventory method affects:

  • Cost of goods sold
  • Gross profit
  • Operating income
  • Income before tax
  • Net income

Under rising prices, FIFO generally results in:

  • Lower cost of goods sold
  • Higher gross profit
  • Higher income before tax
  • Higher current income tax, assuming tax rules follow the financial result

LIFO generally results in:

  • Higher cost of goods sold
  • Lower gross profit
  • Lower income before tax
  • Potentially lower current income tax where permitted

8.5Effect on the Balance Sheet

Under rising prices:

  • FIFO ending inventory is usually higher.
  • LIFO ending inventory is usually lower.
  • A higher inventory balance increases reported current assets.
  • A lower inventory balance decreases reported current assets.

The choice may affect:

  • Working capital
  • Current ratio
  • Inventory turnover
  • Debt covenants
  • Asset-based lending
  • Return-on-assets calculations

8.6Effect on Cash Flow

Inventory methods usually do not change the amount of cash paid to suppliers. However, they can affect cash indirectly through taxes.

Under rising prices, LIFO may produce lower taxable income where permitted. This may reduce current tax payments and increase operating cash flow in the short term.

The total tax effect over the entire life of the inventory may differ from the current-period effect.

9Chapter 9: Inflation and Deflation Scenarios

9.1Inflation

Inflation occurs when the general price level increases over time.

Example purchase costs:

  • First purchase: 10 dollars per unit
  • Second purchase: 12 dollars per unit
  • Third purchase: 14 dollars per unit

Under inflation:

Method Expected cost of goods sold Expected ending inventory Expected gross profit
FIFO Lower Higher Higher
LIFO Higher Lower Lower
Weighted average Middle Middle Middle

9.2Why FIFO Profit Is Usually Higher During Inflation

FIFO assigns older, cheaper costs to cost of goods sold. Current sales revenue is therefore matched with relatively low costs.

This increases reported gross profit.

However, the company may need to pay more than the recorded cost to replace the inventory it has sold. Thus, FIFO profit may not reflect the full economic cost of replacing inventory.

9.3Why LIFO May Reduce Taxes During Inflation

LIFO assigns newer, higher costs to cost of goods sold. This usually reduces accounting profit.

Where tax rules permit LIFO and the financial and tax methods are aligned, the lower profit may reduce current taxable income.

The benefit is often described as a tax-timing benefit rather than a permanent elimination of tax.

9.4Deflation

Deflation occurs when prices decline over time.

Example purchase costs:

  • First purchase: 14 dollars per unit
  • Second purchase: 12 dollars per unit
  • Third purchase: 10 dollars per unit

Under deflation:

Method Expected cost of goods sold Expected ending inventory Expected gross profit
FIFO Higher Lower Lower
LIFO Lower Higher Higher
Weighted average Middle Middle Middle

Under deflation, LIFO may report higher profit because it assigns newer, cheaper costs to cost of goods sold.

9.5Inventory Method and Economic Reality

No inventory method perfectly measures economic profit under all price conditions.

For management purposes, businesses may also monitor:

  • Replacement-cost margins
  • Current-cost income
  • Inventory holding gains
  • Stock turnover
  • Gross margin return on inventory investment

These measures can supplement financial-statement inventory figures.

11Chapter 11: Advantages and Disadvantages in Practice

11.1FIFO

Advantages

  • Simple and understandable
  • Accepted under major accounting frameworks
  • Useful for perishable goods
  • Ending inventory reflects recent prices
  • Easy to integrate with warehouse procedures
  • Reduces the likelihood of old inventory layers

Disadvantages

  • Can increase reported profit during inflation
  • Can increase current tax expense where taxable income follows financial income
  • May not match current replacement costs in cost of goods sold
  • Can make performance look better than the business’s replacement economics

11.2LIFO

Advantages

  • Matches recent costs with current revenue during inflation
  • May reduce taxable income where permitted
  • Can provide a useful margin measure for certain commodities
  • May be suitable for homogeneous, interchangeable inventory

Disadvantages

  • Prohibited under IFRS
  • Complex to administer
  • May leave outdated costs in ending inventory
  • Can cause LIFO liquidation effects
  • Reduces comparability with FIFO users
  • Does not support safe physical stock rotation
  • Can produce a low inventory value during inflation

11.3FEFO

Advantages

  • Protects customers and patients
  • Reduces expired stock
  • Supports recalls and traceability
  • Appropriate for food, medicine, and chemicals
  • Improves warehouse discipline
  • Can reduce waste and disposal costs

Disadvantages

  • Requires accurate expiry information
  • Depends on lot and batch tracking
  • May require specialized software
  • Can increase warehouse handling
  • Does not automatically determine accounting cost
  • Requires clear procedures for damaged or recalled inventory

12Chapter 12: Related Inventory Concepts

12.1Weighted-Average Cost

Weighted-average cost assigns an average cost to units available for sale.

The average cost formula is:

Using the earlier example:

5,500450≈12.22

Weighted average smooths price changes and usually produces results between FIFO and LIFO.

Advantages

  • Simple for interchangeable goods
  • Reduces the impact of extreme price changes
  • Permitted under IFRS and US GAAP
  • Suitable for large volumes of similar products

Disadvantages

  • May not reflect actual unit costs
  • Can obscure recent cost changes
  • Requires consistent calculation procedures

Weighted average may be calculated:

  • Periodically
  • After each purchase under a moving-average system

12.2Specific Identification

Specific identification assigns the actual cost of each identifiable unit to the unit sold.

It is suitable when items are:

  • High-value
  • Unique
  • Easily traceable
  • Not interchangeable

Examples include:

  • Automobiles
  • Jewelry
  • Custom machinery
  • Real estate
  • Aircraft
  • Artwork

Specific identification is less suitable for large quantities of identical low-value products because tracking each item may be impractical.

12.3Just-in-Time Inventory

Just-in-time, or JIT, inventory management attempts to receive materials shortly before they are needed in production or sale.

Benefits

  • Reduces storage costs
  • Reduces capital tied up in inventory
  • Reduces obsolescence
  • Encourages efficient supplier relationships
  • Can improve production flow

Risks

  • Supplier delays can stop production
  • Transportation problems can create shortages
  • Quality problems may have immediate effects
  • Natural disasters and geopolitical events can disrupt supply

JIT does not replace FIFO, LIFO, or FEFO. A company using JIT still needs an accounting method and physical stock-rotation procedure.

12.4Batch and Lot Tracking

A batch or lot is a group of products made or received under similar conditions.

Lot tracking records information such as:

  • Supplier
  • Purchase order
  • Manufacturing date
  • Expiration date
  • Quantity
  • Storage location
  • Quality status
  • Customer shipment
  • Recall status

Batch tracking is essential in:

  • Pharmaceuticals
  • Food production
  • Chemicals
  • Medical devices
  • Cosmetics
  • Aerospace components

12.5Perishables

Perishable goods lose value or become unsafe over time.

Examples include:

  • Fresh fruit
  • Meat
  • Dairy
  • Vaccines
  • Blood products
  • Prepared meals

Perishable inventory usually requires:

  • FEFO rotation
  • Temperature monitoring
  • Expiration alerts
  • Quality inspections
  • Short replenishment cycles
  • Disposal procedures

12.6Obsolescence

Obsolescence occurs when inventory loses value because it is no longer useful, fashionable, compatible, or saleable.

Examples include:

  • Outdated mobile-phone components
  • Seasonal clothing
  • Old computer parts
  • Products replaced by a new model
  • Printed materials with obsolete information

FIFO can reduce the risk of obsolescence when older products are physically sold first. FEFO can reduce the risk when the primary concern is expiration.

12.7Safety Stock

Safety stock is additional inventory held to protect against:

  • Demand uncertainty
  • Supplier delays
  • Transportation disruption
  • Production variability
  • Forecasting errors

Safety stock must still be controlled for age, expiration, and obsolescence.

12.8Reorder Point

A reorder point is the inventory level at which a new order should be placed.

A basic formula is:

For example, if average daily demand is 50 units, supplier lead time is 6 days, and safety stock is 100 units:

(50×6)+100=400 units

The business may place a new order when inventory reaches 400 units.

13Chapter 13: Industry Applications

13.1Pharmacies and Hospitals

Pharmacies and hospitals commonly use FEFO because medicines may have:

  • Expiration dates
  • Batch numbers
  • Storage requirements
  • Recall risks
  • Patient-safety implications

A pharmacy may use:

  • FEFO for physical issuing
  • Specific identification for controlled or high-value products
  • FIFO or weighted average for financial valuation
  • Barcode scanning for batch verification

A medicine should not be issued merely because it is physically closest to the warehouse worker. The system should identify the appropriate batch and confirm its expiration and quality status.

13.2Food and Beverage

Food businesses often use FEFO or FIFO.

FEFO is especially important when batches have different shelf lives. FIFO may be suitable where production and expiration dates generally follow receipt order.

Food businesses should monitor:

  • Use-by dates
  • Best-before dates
  • Temperature
  • Allergen information
  • Supplier lots
  • Product recalls
  • Damaged packaging

13.3Oil and Gas

Oil and gas businesses may hold large quantities of interchangeable commodities and supplies.

Possible methods include:

  • LIFO under US GAAP, where appropriate
  • FIFO
  • Weighted average
  • Specific identification for major equipment
  • Batch or tank-level tracking for quality and regulatory purposes

Oil and gas companies must distinguish between:

  • Accounting valuation
  • Physical measurement
  • Product quality
  • Tank movements
  • Ownership rights

13.4Electronics

Electronics are vulnerable to technological obsolescence.

A product received first may not be the product that becomes obsolete first. Therefore, electronics companies may use:

  • FIFO for physical stock rotation
  • FEFO-like procedures for warranty or shelf-life limitations
  • Specific identification for high-value items
  • Serial-number tracking
  • Weighted average for interchangeable components

Serial-number tracking is important for:

  • Warranty claims
  • Returns
  • Product recalls
  • Theft prevention
  • Customer service
  • Regulatory compliance

13.5Manufacturing

Manufacturers may use different approaches for different inventory classes:

Inventory class Possible method
Raw materials FIFO or weighted average
Work in progress Actual or standard cost
Finished goods FIFO, weighted average, or LIFO where permitted
Unique machinery Specific identification
Expiring chemicals FEFO for physical issue

A single company may use more than one operational method while maintaining a consistent accounting policy for similar inventories.

14Chapter 14: Accounting Standards and Regulatory Considerations

14.1IFRS and IAS 2

IAS 2, Inventories, provides guidance for inventory recognition and measurement under International Financial Reporting Standards.

IAS 2 generally permits:

  • Specific identification for items that are not ordinarily interchangeable
  • FIFO
  • Weighted-average cost for ordinarily interchangeable items

IAS 2 prohibits the use of LIFO for inventory valuation.

IAS 2 also addresses:

  • Costs included in inventory
  • Net realizable value
  • Write-downs
  • Reversal of certain write-downs
  • Expense recognition
  • Disclosure requirements

14.2US GAAP

US Generally Accepted Accounting Principles permit several inventory cost-flow methods, including:

  • FIFO
  • LIFO
  • Weighted average
  • Specific identification when appropriate

The method must be applied consistently and supported by reliable records.

US GAAP also contains requirements concerning:

  • Lower-of-cost-or-market rules in certain circumstances
  • Lower-of-cost-and-net-realizable-value rules
  • Disclosure of accounting policies
  • LIFO liquidation
  • Inventory write-downs

The precise requirements depend on the facts and the applicable accounting guidance.

14.3LIFO Conformity Rule

In the United States, the LIFO conformity rule generally requires that if a company uses LIFO for income-tax purposes, it must also use LIFO for financial reporting purposes for the same inventory, subject to applicable legal requirements.

This rule limits the ability of a business to use:

  • LIFO for tax reporting
  • FIFO for financial reporting

The purpose is to prevent a company from obtaining the tax benefits of LIFO while reporting higher profits and inventory values using FIFO in its financial statements.

Tax rules can be complex and should be reviewed with a qualified tax professional.

14.4LIFO Reserve

The LIFO reserve measures the difference between inventory valued under LIFO and inventory valued under another method, often FIFO.

A common formula is:

Using the earlier example:

2,100 - 1,600 = 500

The LIFO reserve is 500 dollars.

If the LIFO reserve increases, LIFO cost of goods sold is generally higher than FIFO cost of goods sold for the period.

A company using LIFO may disclose information that permits users to compare its inventory with a non-LIFO basis.

14.5Financial-Statement Effects of the LIFO Reserve

A simplified relationship is:

If the LIFO reserve increases by 500 dollars:

  • LIFO cost of goods sold is generally 500 dollars higher than FIFO cost of goods sold.
  • LIFO gross profit is generally 500 dollars lower than FIFO gross profit before tax effects.
  • LIFO inventory is generally 500 dollars lower than FIFO inventory.

This conversion is simplified and may require adjustment for tax effects and other accounting details.

14.6Consistency and Disclosure

A business should apply its inventory method consistently to inventories of a similar nature and use.

Financial statements should generally disclose:

  • The accounting policy used for inventory
  • The carrying amount of inventory
  • The amount recognized as expense
  • Write-downs and reversals where applicable
  • Significant classifications
  • LIFO information where required

15Chapter 15: Practical Implementation

15.1Establish a Written Inventory Policy

An inventory policy should state:

  • Which valuation method is used
  • Which products use FIFO, LIFO, or weighted average
  • Whether FEFO is used operationally
  • How damaged goods are handled
  • How expired goods are handled
  • How returns are recorded
  • How stock counts are performed
  • Who approves adjustments
  • How inventory write-downs are reviewed

15.2Select Appropriate Software

Inventory software or an enterprise resource planning system should support:

  • Perpetual quantity records
  • Cost layers
  • Purchase receipts
  • Sales issues
  • Batch and lot numbers
  • Expiration dates
  • Serial numbers
  • Barcode scanning
  • Stock transfers
  • Cycle counts
  • Reorder points
  • Audit trails
  • User permissions
  • Financial integration

The software should be configured so that operational issuing rules do not unintentionally conflict with the accounting policy.

15.3Barcode and RFID Systems

Barcodes and radio-frequency identification systems can improve inventory control.

A barcode may identify:

  • Product
  • Quantity
  • Batch
  • Lot
  • Expiration date
  • Location

RFID can allow items to be identified without direct line-of-sight scanning.

Technology helps reduce manual errors but does not eliminate the need for:

  • Staff training
  • Physical verification
  • Data validation
  • System maintenance
  • Exception reporting

15.4Warehouse Layout

A good warehouse layout supports the selected rotation method.

For FIFO:

  • Place older receipts where they can be selected first.
  • Use clear receiving dates.
  • Avoid placing new inventory in front of older inventory.

For FEFO:

  • Organize products by expiration date.
  • Use visible date labels.
  • Create separate locations for short-dated stock.
  • Prevent expired stock from being available for sale.

15.5Receiving Procedures

When inventory arrives, staff should verify:

  • Product code
  • Quantity
  • Purchase order
  • Supplier
  • Batch or lot number
  • Manufacturing date
  • Expiration date
  • Packaging condition
  • Temperature, if applicable
  • Quality status

The receiving record should be entered promptly into the inventory system.

15.6Handling Returns

Returned goods should not automatically be returned to available inventory.

The business should determine whether the goods are:

  • Unopened and saleable
  • Damaged
  • Expired
  • Contaminated
  • Recalled
  • In need of inspection
  • Eligible for resale

Each return should be assigned the correct batch, lot, serial number, and cost information.

15.7Handling Expired or Obsolete Inventory

Expired or obsolete inventory should be:

  1. Identified
  2. Segregated
  3. Blocked from sale
  4. Evaluated for write-down
  5. Approved for disposal, return, or alternative use
  6. Removed from the accounting records when appropriate

A warehouse system should prevent expired products from being selected for customer orders.

15.8Cycle Counting

Cycle counting involves counting selected inventory regularly rather than waiting for one annual count.

High-risk or high-value items may be counted more frequently.

A cycle-count program should consider:

  • Value
  • Usage
  • Theft risk
  • Expiration risk
  • Regulatory importance
  • Historical error rates

16Chapter 16: Audit and Internal-Control Considerations

16.1Existence

Auditors need evidence that recorded inventory actually exists.

Useful procedures include:

  • Physical observation
  • Test counts
  • Warehouse inspection
  • Reconciliation to inventory records
  • Confirmation with third-party warehouses

16.2Completeness

All inventory owned by the business should be included.

Auditors may examine:

  • Receiving reports
  • Purchase invoices
  • Shipping documents
  • Goods-in-transit records
  • Consignment arrangements
  • Cutoff around the reporting date

16.3Rights and Obligations

A business must determine whether it owns the inventory.

Inventory may be:

  • Held on consignment
  • Stored for another company
  • Purchased but not yet received
  • Sold but still in the warehouse
  • Subject to retention-of-title clauses

Ownership terms can affect whether inventory belongs on the company’s balance sheet.

16.4Valuation

Auditors evaluate whether:

  • The correct method was applied
  • Unit costs agree with invoices
  • Cost layers are accurate
  • Standard costs are periodically reviewed
  • Damaged inventory is written down
  • Expired inventory is excluded or written down
  • Net realizable value is reasonable

16.5Cutoff

Cutoff ensures that purchases and sales are recorded in the correct accounting period.

Important documents include:

  • Last goods-received note before year-end
  • First goods-received note after year-end
  • Last shipping document before year-end
  • First shipping document after year-end
  • Purchase invoices
  • Sales invoices

Incorrect cutoff can overstate or understate both purchases and inventory.

16.6Inventory Counts

A physical count should include procedures for:

  • Counting teams
  • Independent review
  • Damaged goods
  • Obsolete goods
  • Expired products
  • Goods in transit
  • Goods held for others
  • Count sheets
  • Variance investigation
  • Final reconciliation

16.7IT Controls

Important technology controls include:

  • User access restrictions
  • Approval for cost changes
  • Audit logs
  • Backup procedures
  • Change-management controls
  • Interface reconciliations
  • Master-data controls
  • Barcode validation
  • Expiration-date alerts

Problem 1

FIFO Periodic Inventory

A business has:

  • Beginning inventory: 200 units at 5 dollars
  • Purchase: 300 units at 6 dollars
  • Purchase: 100 units at 8 dollars
  • Units sold: 400

Calculate FIFO cost of goods sold and ending inventory.

●Solution

Total units available:

200 + 300 + 100 = 600

Ending units:

600 - 400 = 200

Under FIFO, the first 400 units sold are:

  • 200 units at 5 dollars
  • 200 units at 6 dollars

Cost of goods sold:

(200×5)+(200×6)=1,000+1,200=2,200

Ending inventory:

  • Remaining 100 units at 6 dollars
  • 100 units at 8 dollars
(100×6)+(100×8)=600+800=1,400
✓Answer
  • FIFO cost of goods sold: 2,200 dollars
  • FIFO ending inventory: 1,400 dollars

Ending inventory consists of:

Problem 8

Choosing Between FIFO and FEFO

A food distributor receives:

  • 500 cartons in April that expire in September

18Chapter 18: Decision Guide

18.1When FIFO May Be Appropriate

Choose FIFO when:

  • Products are perishable
  • Older products should be sold first
  • Compliance with IFRS is required
  • Ending inventory should reflect recent costs
  • The business wants a simple and widely understood method

18.2When LIFO May Be Appropriate

Consider LIFO only when:

  • The applicable accounting framework permits it
  • The business operates in a jurisdiction where it is allowed
  • The business has suitable interchangeable inventory
  • The tax and financial-reporting consequences are understood
  • The business can maintain reliable LIFO records
  • Management understands the effect of old inventory layers

LIFO should not be used as a physical rotation method for food, medicine, or other products that can expire.

18.3When FEFO Is Essential

Use FEFO when:

  • Products expire
  • Product safety is important
  • Batches have different expiration dates
  • Recalls must be managed
  • Regulations require traceability
  • Waste from expiration would be costly

18.4Combining Methods

A business may use different methods for different purposes:

Purpose Possible approach
Physical issue of medicines FEFO
Financial valuation under IFRS FIFO or weighted average
Unique machinery Specific identification
US tax reporting for permitted inventory LIFO
General warehouse rotation FIFO
High-volume identical products Weighted average

The business should document the relationship between operational and financial methods.

19Chapter 19: Key Takeaways

  1. FIFO assigns the oldest costs to cost of goods sold first.
  2. LIFO assigns the newest costs to cost of goods sold first.
  3. FEFO issues products with the earliest expiration dates first.
  4. FIFO and LIFO are accounting cost-flow methods.
  5. FEFO is mainly a physical inventory-rotation method.
  6. During inflation, FIFO usually produces lower cost of goods sold and higher profit than LIFO.
  7. During inflation, LIFO usually produces higher cost of goods sold and lower profit than FIFO.
  8. FIFO is permitted under IFRS and US GAAP.
  9. LIFO is prohibited under IFRS but permitted under US GAAP, subject to applicable requirements.
  10. Weighted average smooths changes in purchase costs.
  11. Specific identification is appropriate for unique, high-value items.
  12. FEFO is critical for pharmaceuticals, food, chemicals, and other expiring products.
  13. Accurate batch, lot, barcode, and expiration records improve inventory control.
  14. Inventory valuation affects the income statement, balance sheet, taxes, and performance ratios.
  15. A business must apply its selected accounting policy consistently and maintain adequate documentation.
