1Introduction

Bin location is the organized identification, allocation, recording, and management of specific storage positions within a warehouse, distribution center, stockroom, or materials-handling facility. A bin location may be a shelf compartment, pallet position, floor-marked area, rack level, drawer, cabinet, cage, yard position, or any other defined place where inventory is stored.

An effective bin-location system connects three elements:

  1. The physical storage position
  2. The inventory item assigned to that position
  3. The information system that records the relationship

The system enables employees and automated equipment to determine:

  • Where an item should be stored
  • Where an item is currently located
  • How much stock is available at that location
  • Which location should be visited for picking
  • Whether the storage position is suitable for the item
  • How inventory movements should be recorded and controlled

Bin location is not merely a labeling activity. It is a combination of warehouse design, inventory control, information management, materials handling, occupational safety, and operational decision-making.

02

2Fundamental Concept

A warehouse contains many storage positions. Without a logical location system, employees must depend on memory, paper records, or repeated searching. This increases travel time, picking errors, misplaced stock, congestion, inventory discrepancies, and safety risks.

A bin-location system assigns a unique identifier to every usable storage position. The identifier normally describes the physical sequence of the position, such as:

Building–Aisle–Rack–Level–Position

An example may be:

WH1-A03-R02-L04-P06

This could represent:

  • WH1: Warehouse 1
  • A03: Aisle 3
  • R02: Rack 2
  • L04: Level 4
  • P06: Position 6

The location code should be understandable, unique, durable, visible, and compatible with the warehouse management system.

2.1Basic Relationship

The essential relationship is:

Whenever stock is received, moved, picked, counted, returned, quarantined, or dispatched, this relationship must be updated accurately.

2.2Bin, Location, Slot, and Storage Position

These terms are related but may have different meanings:

Term Meaning

Rack position A specific place on a rack or shelving structure

Staging location A temporary area for receiving, packing, dispatch, or internal movement
Quarantine location A controlled area for damaged, disputed, expired, or nonconforming stock
Virtual location A system-defined location that represents stock not physically stored in a normal bin, such as goods in transit or under inspection

3Definition

Bin location is a structured method of identifying, assigning, monitoring, and controlling specific storage positions for inventory within a warehouse or materials-management system.

A complete bin-location system generally includes:

  • Location identification codes
  • Physical signs or labels
  • A warehouse layout
  • Storage rules
  • Item-location assignments
  • Capacity data
  • Inventory transaction records
  • Replenishment instructions
  • Counting procedures
  • Exception-handling rules
  • Safety and compliance controls

4Meaning and Scope

Bin location has both a physical and an informational meaning.

4.1Physical Meaning

Physically, bin location determines where material is placed. It considers:

  • Storage equipment
  • Aisle arrangement
  • Rack height
  • Floor capacity
  • Item dimensions
  • Weight
  • Hazard classification
  • Environmental requirements
  • Accessibility
  • Handling equipment
  • Security needs

4.2Informational Meaning

Informationally, bin location provides a common language for warehouse personnel and digital systems. It enables the organization to answer:

  • What is stored here?
  • How much is stored here?
  • When was it received?
  • Which lot or serial number is present?
  • Is the stock available, reserved, damaged, or quarantined?
  • Who moved it?
  • Where should the next unit be placed?
  • Which route should a picker follow?

4.3Operational Scope

Bin location affects:

  • Receiving
  • Putaway
  • Storage
  • Replenishment
  • Order picking
  • Packing
  • Dispatch
  • Returns
  • Cycle counting
  • Inventory reconciliation
  • Production material supply
  • Maintenance stores
  • Spare-parts management
  • Yard and container management

5Principles of Bin Location

5.1Uniqueness

Every active storage position should have one unique identifier. Duplicate codes create ambiguity and can cause stock to be recorded in the wrong place.

5.2Visibility

Labels should be visible from the normal working position of the employee or equipment operator. Labels must remain readable under the warehouse lighting conditions.

5.3Consistency

The same coding logic should be used throughout the facility. A location code should follow a predictable sequence rather than using unrelated abbreviations.

5.4Logical Sequence

Locations should normally be numbered in a logical physical direction, such as:

  • Left to right
  • Front to back
  • Lower level to upper level
  • Receiving area to dispatch area

A consistent sequence reduces search time and training difficulty.

5.5Accessibility

Frequently picked products should be placed where they can be reached quickly and safely. Heavy or awkward items should not be placed in locations that require unsafe lifting or difficult handling.

5.6Compatibility

The location must be suitable for the item's:

  • Weight
  • Dimensions
  • Shape
  • Packaging
  • Handling requirements
  • Hazard classification
  • Temperature requirement
  • Security level
  • Rotation pattern

5.7Traceability

The system should record inventory by item, location, quantity, lot, batch, serial number, status, and movement history whenever applicable.

5.8Capacity Control

A location should not be assigned more inventory than it can safely and practically hold.

5.9Flexibility

The system should allow changes caused by seasonal demand, new products, revised packaging, facility expansion, and changes in order profiles.

5.10Accuracy at the Point of Movement

Inventory records should be updated when the movement occurs, not several hours or days later. Delayed recording creates a difference between system stock and physical stock.

5.11Separation of Stock Conditions

Good stock, damaged stock, expired stock, returned stock, customer-owned stock, samples, and quarantined material should have clearly separated locations or status controls.

5.12Safety Before Convenience

A location should never be selected solely because it reduces travel distance if it creates a risk of collapse, collision, contamination, injury, fire, or unauthorized access.

06

6Objectives

The principal objectives of bin location are to:

  1. Reduce the time required to find inventory.
  2. Improve inventory record accuracy.
  3. Increase storage capacity without compromising safety.
  4. Reduce travel distance for warehouse employees and equipment.
  5. Improve picking productivity.
  6. Support stock rotation and traceability.
  7. Prevent unauthorized or unsuitable storage.
  8. Simplify receiving and putaway decisions.
  9. Improve replenishment performance.
  10. Support cycle counting and auditing.
  11. Reduce damage, loss, and misplacement.
  12. Provide reliable data for planning and decision-making.
  13. Improve customer-service performance.
  14. Integrate physical operations with WMS or ERP systems.
  15. Create accountability for every inventory movement.

7Purpose

A bin-location system serves several connected purposes.

7.1Storage Purpose

It ensures that each item has an appropriate place based on its physical and operational characteristics.

7.2Retrieval Purpose

It enables employees or automated systems to locate and retrieve material quickly.

7.3Control Purpose

It prevents inventory from becoming unidentified, mixed, inaccessible, or incorrectly recorded.

7.4Planning Purpose

It provides location and capacity data for purchasing, replenishment, labor planning, and warehouse expansion.

7.5Compliance Purpose

It supports traceability, segregation, safe storage, stock rotation, and regulatory requirements.

7.6Service Purpose

It improves the ability to deliver complete and accurate orders on time.

8Importance and Significance

8.1Operational Importance

A well-designed bin-location system reduces unnecessary walking, forklift travel, searching, rehandling, and manual checking.

8.2Financial Importance

Improved location control can reduce:

  • Labor cost
  • Inventory shrinkage
  • Product damage
  • Expediting cost
  • Obsolescence
  • Excess safety stock
  • Space requirements
  • Cost of inventory discrepancies

8.3Customer-Service Importance

Accurate locations support:

  • Higher order accuracy
  • Faster dispatch
  • Better availability information
  • More dependable delivery promises
  • Fewer short shipments
  • Faster response to customer inquiries

8.4Safety Importance

Proper location assignment prevents:

  • Overloaded racks
  • Blocked emergency routes
  • Unsafe lifting
  • Incompatible chemical storage
  • Falling products
  • Forklift congestion
  • Uncontrolled stacking

8.5Strategic Importance

Location data supports warehouse redesign, automation, network planning, product rationalization, and continuous improvement.

9Good and Bad Bin Location Systems

9.1Characteristics of a Good System

A good bin-location system is:

  • Accurate
  • Simple to understand
  • Scalable
  • Visible
  • Safe
  • Flexible
  • Traceable
  • Integrated with business systems
  • Suitable for the facility layout
  • Based on measurable operating requirements

9.2Characteristics of a Poor System

A poor system may contain:

  • Duplicate location identifiers
  • Missing labels
  • Unrecorded temporary locations
  • Inconsistent numbering
  • Mixed products without controls
  • Overfilled storage positions
  • Unclear quarantine areas
  • Inaccurate capacity information
  • Frequent system overrides
  • Stock stored in aisles or walkways
  • Reliance on employee memory
  • No formal procedure for location changes

9.3Comparison

A location structure may use the following hierarchy:

Site›Building›Zone›Aisle›Bay›Rack›Level›Position

Not every warehouse requires every level. The structure should reflect the facility's complexity.

10.3Recommended Coding Rules

  • Use a fixed format.
  • Avoid ambiguous characters where possible.
  • Distinguish letters and numbers clearly.
  • Keep code segments in a consistent order.
  • Define the direction of numbering.
  • Reserve special prefixes for staging, quarantine, returns, and damage.
  • Avoid changing location codes without a controlled migration plan.
  • Maintain a current location master file.

10.4Location Master Data

Each location record may include:

  • Location code
  • Description
  • Zone
  • Storage type
  • Length
  • Width
  • Height
  • Maximum volume
  • Maximum weight
  • Equipment restriction
  • Temperature class
  • Hazard class
  • Allowed item categories
  • Current status
  • Occupancy
  • Cycle-count frequency
  • Pick or reserve designation

12Bin Location Methodologies

12.1Dedicated Storage

Under dedicated storage, each item is assigned a permanent location or defined set of locations.

Advantages

  • Easy to understand
  • Simple for manual operations
  • Fast identification for familiar employees
  • Stable placement of frequently used items
  • Useful for controlled environments

Disadvantages

  • Empty space may remain unused
  • Requires more total space
  • Performs poorly when demand changes significantly
  • Can cause congestion if product sizes vary

Suitable Applications

  • Small parts stores
  • Maintenance inventory
  • High-value products
  • Regulated products
  • Stable manufacturing materials
  • Items requiring permanent segregation

12.2Random or Dynamic Storage

Under random storage, inventory is placed in any suitable available location, and the system records the exact position.

Advantages

  • High space utilization
  • Effective for variable inventory levels
  • Adapts to seasonal changes
  • Reduces unused dedicated space

Disadvantages

  • Requires accurate real-time records
  • More dependent on WMS support
  • Misplaced items become difficult to locate
  • Employees need stronger scanning and transaction discipline

Suitable Applications

  • Large distribution centers
  • Broad product ranges
  • Barcode- or RFID-enabled operations
  • Facilities with changing inventory volumes

12.3Class-Based Storage

Class-based storage divides inventory into groups and assigns each group a storage area. Items within the class may be assigned dynamically.

Typical classes include:

  • Fast-moving
  • Medium-moving
  • Slow-moving
  • High-value
  • Hazardous
  • Temperature-sensitive
  • Oversized
  • Fragile
  • Secure
  • Seasonal

Benefits

Class-based storage provides a balance between the simplicity of dedicated storage and the capacity of random storage.

12.4ABC-Based Storage

ABC analysis ranks items according to annual consumption value or another selected measure.

A common interpretation is:

  • A items: High-impact items requiring close control
  • B items: Medium-impact items requiring normal control
  • C items: Lower-impact items managed with simpler procedures

ABC classification may influence location as follows:

Class Typical Location Strategy
A Near dispatch, at ergonomic height, in highly accessible pick faces
B Moderate-access locations
C Higher, lower, or more distant locations if safe and practical

ABC classification should not be based solely on cost. A low-cost item may be critical to production, and a high-cost item may have low transaction frequency. Criticality, demand frequency, order-line frequency, size, hazard, and service consequences should also be considered.

12.6Family Grouping

Related products are stored near one another. Examples include:

  • Components used in the same production process
  • Products commonly ordered together
  • Accessories associated with a main product
  • Spare parts for the same equipment family

Family grouping can reduce search time, but it should not create congestion or violate compatibility requirements.

12.7Commodity-Based Storage

Products are arranged by material type or product category, such as:

  • Metals
  • Electrical components
  • Packaging materials
  • Finished goods
  • Cleaning chemicals
  • Spare parts

This method supports employees who specialize in a product category but may increase travel if demand patterns are not considered.

16Relationship with Different Industry Facets

16.1Manufacturing

Bin location supports line-side supply, component traceability, kanban replenishment, work-in-process control, and maintenance stores.

Important considerations include:

  • Production sequence
  • Bill of materials
  • Batch and lot control
  • Line-side ergonomics
  • Material shortages
  • Return of unused components
  • Separation of approved and rejected material

16.2Retail

Retail warehouses use bin location to support store replenishment, e-commerce orders, promotional peaks, returns, and product variety.

Important considerations include:

  • Order-line frequency
  • Seasonal demand
  • Product presentation
  • Case and each picking
  • Fast-moving consumer goods
  • Promotional stock
  • Omnichannel fulfillment

16.3E-Commerce

E-commerce warehouses often process many small orders with a high number of order lines.

Bin location affects:

  • Batch picking
  • Wave picking
  • Zone picking
  • Single-item orders
  • Returns
  • Packing-station replenishment
  • Same-day fulfillment

16.4Pharmaceuticals and Healthcare

These environments require strict control of:

  • Lot and batch numbers
  • Expiration dates
  • Temperature
  • Quarantine
  • Product recall
  • Controlled substances
  • Access authorization
  • First-expiry-first-out rotation

16.5Food and Beverage

Location systems must support:

  • Food safety
  • Temperature control
  • Allergen segregation
  • Expiration management
  • First-expiry-first-out rotation
  • Sanitation
  • Product recall
  • Pest control

16.6Automotive

Automotive operations depend on reliable component availability and sequence accuracy.

Bin location supports:

  • Line-side delivery
  • Supplier packaging
  • Returnable containers
  • Model-specific components
  • High-volume small parts
  • Production synchronization

16.7Construction and Building Materials

The system must accommodate:

  • Long materials
  • Heavy products
  • Outdoor storage
  • Weather exposure
  • Variable shapes
  • Crane and forklift handling
  • Project-specific inventory

16.8Spare Parts and Maintenance Stores

Criticality may be more important than transaction frequency. A slow-moving part can be essential if equipment failure would stop production.

Location decisions should consider:

  • Equipment criticality
  • Replacement urgency
  • Part compatibility
  • Shelf life
  • Security
  • Technical documentation
  • Obsolescence risk

16.9Chemicals and Hazardous Materials

Location assignment must comply with segregation rules, containment requirements, labeling, ventilation, emergency response, and access restrictions.

16.10Cold Chain

Locations must support temperature monitoring, restricted dwell time, insulated handling, power-failure procedures, and product-specific temperature ranges.

16.11Third-Party Logistics

A 3PL may manage inventory for several customers. Bin location must therefore distinguish:

  • Customer ownership
  • Product ownership
  • Service-level requirements
  • Billing records
  • Customer-specific handling rules
  • Shared and dedicated storage

17Need for Bin Location in Industry

Organizations need bin location because inventory complexity grows with:

  • Number of products
  • Number of customers
  • Number of orders
  • Number of storage areas
  • Number of employees
  • Number of shifts
  • Product variation
  • Regulatory requirements
  • Demand volatility
  • Automation level

Without location control, growth often produces informal storage, unreliable inventory data, and increased operating cost.

A bin-location system becomes especially necessary when:

  • Inventory cannot be identified quickly.
  • Employees spend substantial time searching.
  • Orders are frequently short or incorrect.
  • Stock is found in unrecorded places.
  • Receiving and putaway are delayed.
  • Cycle counts show recurring variances.
  • Storage space appears full despite low usable capacity.
  • Product traceability is required.
  • Multiple shifts use the same warehouse.
  • Automated equipment depends on accurate coordinates.

20Slotting Methodology

Slotting is the process of deciding which products should occupy which storage and picking positions.

20.1Slotting Steps

  1. Collect item master data.
  2. Collect order history.
  3. Measure product dimensions and weights.
  4. Identify handling and storage restrictions.
  5. Calculate demand frequency and turnover.
  6. Identify products commonly ordered together.
  7. Map available storage positions.
  8. Classify locations by accessibility and capacity.
  9. Score possible item-location combinations.
  10. Assign products according to approved rules.
  11. Simulate travel, replenishment, and congestion.
  12. Implement changes in controlled stages.
  13. Measure results.
  14. Review the slotting plan periodically.

21Optimization Techniques

21.1ABC and Velocity Analysis

Combine annual consumption value with movement frequency. This avoids placing a high-value but rarely ordered item automatically in the closest location when a frequently ordered lower-value item may create more labor demand.

21.2Family and Affinity Analysis

Analyze order history to identify products frequently ordered together. Place compatible products near one another where doing so does not increase congestion or create regulatory problems.

21.3Pick-Face and Reserve Separation

Create accessible forward pick locations and bulk reserve locations. Replenishment should occur during suitable time windows so that picking is not interrupted.

21.4Ergonomic Slotting

Place frequently picked products between approximately knee and shoulder height where feasible. Heavy items should generally remain at lower levels, subject to equipment and safety requirements.

21.5Seasonal Re-Slotting

Move seasonal products closer to active pick areas before predictable demand peaks. Return them to a more suitable long-term position after the season.

21.6Golden-Zone Design

The golden zone is the area where products can be handled with relatively low bending, reaching, or stretching. It is especially important for high-frequency manual picking.

21.7Travel-Path Reduction

Use one-way routes, logical aisle numbering, zone sequencing, and pick-path analysis to reduce unnecessary movement.

21.8Congestion Management

Do not place every fast-moving item in the same area. Distribute high-demand products according to order patterns, equipment capacity, labor availability, and aisle congestion.

21.9Multi-Objective Optimization

A location plan should balance:

  • Travel distance
  • Space utilization
  • Replenishment effort
  • Safety
  • Order accuracy
  • Ergonomics
  • Capital investment
  • Future flexibility

The closest location is not always the best location.

21.10Simulation

Before implementing major changes, organizations may model:

  • Order volumes
  • Travel routes
  • Equipment use
  • Queue formation
  • Replenishment timing
  • Seasonal peaks
  • Labor requirements

21.11Continuous Improvement

Use a cycle such as:

1Measure
2Analyze
3Identify improvement opportunity
4Test change
5Implement
6Verify results
7Standardize or revise

22Warehouse Management Systems and Technology

22.1Warehouse Management System

A WMS can:

  • Maintain the location master
  • Recommend putaway locations
  • Direct picking tasks
  • Control replenishment
  • Record stock movements
  • Support lot and serial tracking
  • Manage cycle counts
  • Enforce status restrictions
  • Provide real-time inventory visibility
  • Report performance

A WMS is effective only when master data, scanning behavior, physical labels, and operating procedures are reliable.

22.2Barcode Scanning

Barcodes provide a practical method for identifying products and locations. A typical transaction may require:

  1. Scan the employee or task.
  2. Scan the source location.
  3. Scan the item.
  4. Enter or scan the quantity.
  5. Scan the destination location.
  6. Confirm the transaction.

22.3Radio-Frequency Identification

RFID can identify tagged items, pallets, containers, or locations without requiring direct line-of-sight scanning. It may improve speed and visibility, but it requires appropriate tag placement, reader coverage, data controls, and investment analysis.

22.4Voice-Directed Picking

Voice systems provide spoken instructions and may collect verbal confirmations. They can reduce the need to handle paper or handheld devices, although noise, language, training, and recognition accuracy must be evaluated.

22.5Pick-to-Light and Put-to-Light

Lights identify required quantities at storage positions. These systems can be effective in high-volume, repetitive operations.

22.6Automated Storage and Retrieval Systems

ASRS equipment stores and retrieves goods automatically. It requires:

  • Accurate dimensions
  • Reliable location coordinates
  • Equipment-compatible loads
  • Preventive maintenance
  • Emergency procedures
  • System redundancy or recovery planning

22.7Autonomous Mobile Robots

Mobile robots may transport totes, shelves, pallets, or orders. Bin locations must be digitally mapped, physically accessible, and maintained within operating tolerances.

22.8Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning

AI-supported slotting can analyze:

  • Historical demand
  • Seasonality
  • Product affinity
  • Travel patterns
  • Replenishment events
  • Congestion
  • Forecast changes
  • Space constraints

AI recommendations should remain subject to human review, safety rules, data-quality checks, and explainable decision criteria.

22.9Digital Twins

A digital twin can represent the warehouse layout, inventory, equipment, and process flows in a digital environment. It can support simulation and scenario testing.

22.10Internet of Things

Sensors can monitor:

  • Temperature
  • Humidity
  • Door status
  • Equipment movement
  • Location occupancy
  • Vibration
  • Power conditions
  • Environmental alarms

23Safety and Compliance

Bin location must comply with applicable laws, regulations, standards, and internal procedures. Requirements vary by country, industry, product, building type, and storage equipment.

23.1General Safety Requirements

  • Keep aisles and emergency exits clear.
  • Observe rack load limits.
  • Inspect racks and storage equipment.
  • Prevent unstable stacking.
  • Control access to elevated areas.
  • Use suitable ladders and lifting equipment.
  • Separate pedestrians and powered equipment where required.
  • Maintain appropriate lighting.
  • Display hazard and capacity signs.
  • Store heavy products at safe levels.
  • Control incompatible materials.
  • Maintain fire protection clearances.
  • Investigate near misses.

23.2Hazardous Materials

Hazardous materials require:

  • Correct classification
  • Legible labels
  • Safety data sheets
  • Segregation of incompatible substances
  • Spill containment
  • Ventilation where required
  • Emergency equipment
  • Access control
  • Staff training
  • Inspection and disposal procedures

23.3Food and Pharmaceutical Compliance

These environments may require:

  • Lot and batch traceability
  • Expiration control
  • Temperature monitoring
  • Sanitation procedures
  • Pest control
  • Quarantine
  • Recall capability
  • Documented stock rotation
  • Controlled access

23.4Rack and Structural Safety

Location assignment must consider:

  • Rated capacity
  • Beam configuration
  • Upright condition
  • Floor loading
  • Pallet condition
  • Load stability
  • Impact protection
  • Inspection intervals

23.5Compliance Documentation

Records may include:

  • Inspection forms
  • Training records
  • Location approval lists
  • Hazard assessments
  • Temperature records
  • Product release records
  • Incident reports
  • Corrective-action records
  • Calibration records
  • Maintenance documents

24Ethics in Bin Location Management

Ethical warehouse management requires accurate, fair, safe, and responsible decisions.

24.1Accuracy and Honesty

Employees should not conceal shortages, invent movements, backdate transactions, or manipulate records to meet performance targets.

24.2Worker Well-Being

Productivity targets should not encourage unsafe lifting, excessive speed, skipped breaks, or the use of damaged equipment.

24.3Data Privacy

System records may include employee identities, movement histories, productivity information, and customer data. Access should be limited to legitimate business needs.

24.4Fair Use of Automation

Automation should be introduced with appropriate training, role redesign, consultation, and consideration of worker safety.

24.5Responsible Performance Measurement

KPIs should not reward one result while causing hidden damage elsewhere. For example, increasing picks per hour should not reduce accuracy or increase injuries.

24.6Environmental Responsibility

Location planning can reduce energy consumption, unnecessary travel, packaging waste, product damage, and avoidable disposal.

24.7Conflict of Interest

Location decisions should be based on operational, safety, and customer requirements rather than personal preference, favoritism, or improper commercial influence.

25Risks, Errors, and Mistakes

25.1Common Risks

  • Incorrect location assignment
  • Wrong item stored in a bin
  • Mixed lots without authorization
  • Unrecorded movement
  • Duplicate location codes
  • Incorrect quantity confirmation
  • Damaged label
  • Overfilled bin
  • Stock placed in an aisle
  • Uncontrolled temporary storage
  • Expired inventory not identified
  • Wrong unit of measure
  • Product substitution without approval
  • Inaccurate dimensions in the item master
  • Incorrect rack capacity
  • Inadequate system permissions
  • Failure of scanners or networks
  • Untrained temporary labor
  • Incomplete handover between shifts

25.2Causes of Errors

Errors may arise from:

  • Poor layout
  • Ambiguous labels
  • Similar-looking products
  • Inadequate lighting
  • Rushed work
  • Weak training
  • Poor master data
  • System downtime
  • Excessive product variety
  • Inconsistent procedures
  • Poor supervision
  • Poorly designed incentives
  • Lack of cycle counting
  • Inadequate exception handling

25.3Error-Prevention Controls

  • Scan both source and destination locations.
  • Use product images where useful.
  • Separate similar items.
  • Apply independent verification for high-risk transactions.
  • Use clear labels and color coding.
  • Maintain controlled temporary locations.
  • Require reason codes for adjustments.
  • Perform cycle counts based on risk.
  • Review recurring exceptions.
  • Restrict system overrides.
  • Train employees during onboarding and periodically thereafter.
  • Maintain backup procedures for system outages.

25.4Error-Correction Process

1Identify discrepancy
2Secure affected inventory
3Check recent transactions and documents
4Perform physical verification
5Determine root cause
6Correct stock and location records with authorization
7Assess impact on orders, production, quality, and compliance
8Apply corrective action
9Monitor for recurrence

26Detailed Case Study: Improving Bin Location in a Distribution Center

26.1Background

A medium-sized distribution center handles household maintenance products for regional retail stores and online customers. The facility has:

  • Approximately 5,000 active stock-keeping units
  • Pallet racks and manual shelving
  • Two daily shifts
  • Retail replenishment orders
  • E-commerce each-pick orders
  • A barcode-enabled WMS
  • Seasonal demand variation
  • Separate receiving, storage, picking, packing, and dispatch areas

26.2Initial Problems

The organization experienced:

  • Frequent picker searches
  • Stock stored in unrecorded overflow positions
  • High replenishment activity during peak picking periods
  • Similar products located together without sufficient separation
  • Slow-moving inventory occupying prime pick locations
  • Inconsistent labels
  • High cycle-count variance in small-item shelving
  • Congestion near the dispatch area

26.3Baseline Review

The management team collected:

  • Order-line history for twelve months
  • Item dimensions and weights
  • Inventory turnover
  • Pick frequency
  • Replenishment records
  • Travel observations
  • Location capacity
  • Error reports
  • Safety observations
  • Product affinity data

The review showed that a relatively small group of products generated a large proportion of order lines. However, several of those products were located in distant or poorly labeled areas.

26.4Improvement Method

The team used a combined methodology:

  1. ABC classification based on annual consumption value.
  2. Velocity analysis based on order-line frequency.
  3. Family grouping for compatible products.
  4. Ergonomic review of manual pick positions.
  5. Separation of pick faces and reserve storage.
  6. Standardized location labels.
  7. Scanning of both item and location.
  8. Controlled overflow locations.
  9. Scheduled slotting reviews before seasonal peaks.

26.5New Location Rules

  • High-frequency, lightweight items were placed in accessible pick faces.
  • Heavy cases were assigned to lower rack levels.
  • Fast-moving items were distributed to reduce congestion.
  • Similar products received additional visual identification.
  • Expiration-controlled products were placed in locations supporting stock rotation.
  • Reserve stock was separated from forward pick stock.
  • Overflow storage required a system transaction and defined expiration date.
  • Damaged, returned, and quarantined goods were physically segregated.

26.6Implementation Flow

1Clean item and location master data
2Measure current performance
3Classify inventory and analyze order patterns
4Design new slotting plan
5Verify safety and equipment compatibility
6Label and map locations
7Move inventory in controlled waves
8Scan every movement
9Cycle count affected areas
10Train employees
11Monitor KPIs
12Adjust the plan

26.7Results

After implementation, management observed:

  • Lower average travel per pick
  • Fewer location searches
  • Better visibility of overflow stock
  • Improved separation of product conditions
  • Faster replenishment response
  • Fewer picking errors
  • More consistent cycle-count results
  • Reduced congestion during peak periods

The organization did not treat the initial improvement as permanent. It established a quarterly slotting review and a pre-season review for major demand changes.

26.8Lessons Learned

  1. A WMS cannot compensate for inaccurate physical practices.
  2. High-value items and high-frequency items are not always the same.
  3. Storage capacity must be measured using safe and usable capacity.
  4. Labels are an operational control, not merely a visual aid.
  5. Overflow locations require the same discipline as normal locations.
  6. Slotting should consider replenishment, not only picking.
  7. Worker input can reveal practical problems that transaction data does not show.
  8. Safety and productivity should be designed together.
  9. Location changes should be implemented in controlled stages.
  10. Performance improvement requires ongoing review.

27Practical Examples

1
Example 1

Fast-Moving Small Product

A small electrical connector is ordered frequently, weighs very little, and is commonly picked as an individual unit.

Recommended location
  • Near the main pick path
  • At an ergonomic height
  • In a clearly labeled small-parts bin
  • Near related connectors
  • With a reserve quantity nearby
  • With lot control if required
2
Example 2

Heavy Case Product

A cleaning product is sold in heavy cases and has moderate demand.

Recommended location
  • Lower rack level
  • Suitable pallet or case position
  • Away from fragile products
  • With sufficient aisle clearance
  • With a defined maximum stack height
3
Example 3

Expiration-Controlled Product

A medical supply has lot and expiration requirements.

Recommended location
  • Approved clean storage area
  • Lot-controlled bin
  • Clearly separated from expired or quarantined stock
  • System configured for first-expiry-first-out selection
  • Access restricted to trained personnel
4
Example 4

Critical Spare Part

A replacement bearing is rarely requested but is essential for a production machine.

Recommended location
  • Secure and identifiable location
  • Protected against corrosion and damage
  • Near maintenance stores or an approved secure area
  • Linked to the equipment or bill of materials
  • Included in a critical-spares review
5
Example 5

Seasonal Item

A product has high demand during a short annual season.

Recommended location
  • Re-slot before the season
  • Place in a suitable accessible area during peak demand
  • Maintain reserve capacity
  • Return to a long-term location after the season
  • Review demand forecast and obsolete stock afterward

30Limitations of Bin Location Systems

A bin-location system has important limitations.

30.1Dependence on Data Accuracy

Incorrect item dimensions, weights, units of measure, or demand data can produce poor location decisions.

30.2Technology Dependence

WMS, barcode, RFID, or automation failures may interrupt operations if backup procedures are weak.

30.3Cost

Labels, racks, scanners, software, automation, training, and layout changes require investment.

30.4Change Management

Employees may resist new location rules, especially when existing informal practices are familiar.

30.5Demand Volatility

A location plan based on historical demand may become unsuitable when customer behavior changes.

30.6Physical Constraints

A building may limit aisle width, vertical expansion, fire protection, temperature control, or equipment movement.

30.7Trade-Offs

Improving one objective may weaken another. For example:

  • Higher space utilization may reduce accessibility.
  • Shorter pick travel may increase replenishment travel.
  • Greater product grouping may increase congestion.
  • More automation may increase system complexity.
  • Dedicated storage may improve simplicity but reduce flexibility.

30.8Human Error

Even a well-designed system can fail if employees do not scan, label, confirm, or follow procedures.

33Summary

Bin location is a foundational component of warehousing and materials management. It provides the structure needed to connect physical inventory with accurate information. A strong system uses unique identifiers, logical coding, suitable storage rules, reliable transactions, appropriate technology, safety controls, and regular performance review.

The most effective bin-location strategy is not necessarily the one that uses every available space or places every fast-moving item nearest to dispatch. It is the strategy that balances:

  • Accuracy
  • Accessibility
  • Safety
  • Space utilization
  • Travel
  • Replenishment
  • Traceability
  • Flexibility
  • Cost
  • Customer service

Dedicated, random, class-based, ABC, turnover-based, family-based, and technology-supported methods can all be effective when matched to the organization's products, processes, facility, risks, and service requirements. Continuous measurement and controlled improvement are essential because demand, product mix, technology, and business priorities change over time.
