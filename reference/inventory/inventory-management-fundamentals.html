Inventory Management Fundamentals

A practical industrial reference covering the purpose, types, costs, classification, planning parameters, control systems and performance measures used to manage inventory effectively.

1. Definition

Inventory is the stock of materials, components, spares, consumables, finished goods, packing materials and other items held by an organization to support production, maintenance, operations, sales or other business requirements.

Inventory management is the systematic planning, procurement, receipt, storage, control, issue, replenishment and disposal of materials so that the required material is available at the required time and place while avoiding unnecessary inventory investment.

2. Objectives of Inventory Management

  • Maintain uninterrupted production and operations.
  • Ensure required materials are available when needed.
  • Maintain appropriate service levels.
  • Minimize stock-outs and production interruptions.
  • Control excess and obsolete inventory.
  • Reduce inventory carrying cost.
  • Optimize working capital invested in stock.
  • Improve inventory accuracy.
  • Establish systematic replenishment parameters.
  • Provide reliable inventory information for management decisions.

3. Major Types of Industrial Inventory

Inventory Type Purpose / Example
Raw Material Materials consumed in manufacturing or processing.
Work in Process Material currently undergoing production or processing.
Finished Goods Completed products awaiting dispatch or sale.
MRO Inventory Maintenance, repair and operating items such as bearings, belts, valves and electrical spares.
Consumables Items consumed during operations, maintenance or production.
Packing Materials Materials used for packing, handling and dispatch.
Critical Spares Items whose unavailability may cause significant operational consequences.
Insurance Spares Strategic spares maintained because replacement lead time or failure consequences are significant.

4. Why Organizations Hold Inventory

Inventory may be maintained because of:

  • Demand uncertainty.
  • Supplier lead time.
  • Demand and supply variability.
  • Production requirements.
  • Minimum order quantities.
  • Economic purchasing quantities.
  • Seasonal requirements.
  • Criticality of material.
  • Supplier reliability issues.
  • Transportation constraints.
  • Price or availability considerations.
Management principle: Inventory should not be considered simply as "more is safer". The objective is to determine an appropriate stock level based on demand, lead time, criticality, service requirements, cost and business risk.

5. Inventory Costs

Cost Description
Purchase Cost Basic material acquisition cost.
Ordering / Procurement Cost Administrative and transaction costs associated with placing orders.
Carrying Cost Cost of holding inventory, including capital, storage, insurance, handling and related costs.
Stock-out Cost Cost or consequence of material unavailability.
Obsolescence Cost Loss arising when inventory becomes unusable or commercially obsolete.
Storage Cost Warehouse/store space, handling and preservation expenses.

6. Inventory Classification

Different classification techniques answer different management questions. No single classification method is sufficient for every inventory decision.

Method Primary Basis Typical Management Question
ABC Annual consumption value Which items require stronger value control?
FSN Movement / issue frequency Which items move frequently?
VED Criticality Which items are operationally critical?
HML Unit price Which items have high unit value?
SDE Procurement difficulty Which items are difficult to procure?
XYZ Stock value / variability depending on methodology Which stock positions need special control?

Combined analysis such as ABC-FSN or ABC-VED can provide a more detailed basis for inventory control decisions.

7. Core Inventory Planning Parameters

Parameter Purpose
Minimum Level Lower operational stock boundary used in a practical control system.
Maximum Level Upper stock boundary intended to limit excessive inventory.
Reorder Point Stock position at which replenishment should be initiated.
Safety Stock Buffer maintained against uncertainty in demand and/or supply.
Lead Time Time between initiating replenishment and material availability.
Reorder Quantity Quantity normally ordered when replenishment is triggered.
MOQ Minimum quantity accepted by a supplier.
Review Period Interval at which inventory position is reviewed under a periodic review system.

8. Inventory Control Systems

  • Continuous Review System: inventory position is monitored continuously.
  • Periodic Review System: stock is reviewed at predetermined intervals.
  • Min-Max System: replenishment is controlled using minimum and maximum parameters.
  • Fixed Order Quantity: a predefined quantity is ordered when the trigger point is reached.
  • Fixed Order Interval: inventory is reviewed and replenished at fixed intervals.
  • Two-Bin System: one bin provides working stock while the second acts as replenishment/reserve stock.
  • Three-Bin System: separates working, reserve and replenishment/supply signals according to the operating design.
  • Kanban: uses a visual signal to authorize replenishment in a pull-based system.

9. Important Inventory Formulas

Inventory Turnover Ratio

Inventory Turnover Ratio = Annual Cost of Consumption / Average Inventory

Inventory Days

Inventory Days = Average Inventory / Annual Consumption × 365

Stock Coverage

Stock Coverage = Current Stock / Average Daily Consumption

Basic Reorder Point

ROP = Lead-Time Demand + Safety Stock

The exact formula used in an industrial system should reflect the organization's planning policy, demand pattern, lead-time variation and service requirement.

10. Industrial Example

Suppose a maintenance item has an average consumption of 10 units per day, supplier lead time of 7 days, and a safety stock of 30 units.

Lead-Time Demand = 10 × 7 = 70 units

ROP = 70 + 30 = 100 units

Under this simplified policy, replenishment would be triggered when the relevant inventory position reaches approximately 100 units.

In a real industrial system, open purchase orders, reserved stock, pending receipts, consumption variability, supplier reliability and criticality may also need to be considered.

11. Inventory Management KPIs

Inventory Turnover RatioMeasures how frequently inventory is consumed/replaced over a period.
Inventory DaysIndicates the approximate days of inventory represented by the stock.
Inventory AccuracyMeasures agreement between system stock and physical stock.
Stock-out RateMeasures frequency or proportion of material shortages.
Service LevelMeasures ability to satisfy material requirements.
Excess Inventory %Tracks stock above defined requirements.
Dead Inventory %Tracks inventory with no movement according to the organization's defined criterion.
Stock CoverageIndicates how long current stock may support expected consumption.

12. Management Decision Framework

  1. Identify the material and its business purpose.
  2. Establish accurate item master data.
  3. Analyze historical consumption.
  4. Determine criticality and procurement characteristics.
  5. Analyze lead time and variability.
  6. Select an appropriate inventory classification.
  7. Set planning parameters.
  8. Monitor stock movement continuously or periodically.
  9. Review excess, ageing and non-moving inventory.
  10. Measure performance using relevant KPIs.
  11. Adjust parameters when demand, lead time or operating conditions change.

13. Common Inventory Management Errors

  • Creating duplicate item codes.
  • Using inaccurate or incomplete item descriptions.
  • Setting Min-Max levels without considering actual consumption.
  • Ignoring supplier lead-time variation.
  • Holding critical and non-critical items using identical policies.
  • Ignoring non-moving and obsolete inventory.
  • Relying entirely on system stock without physical verification.
  • Purchasing only because the current price appears attractive.
  • Failing to review open purchase orders before creating new requirements.
  • Using a single inventory classification for every decision.
