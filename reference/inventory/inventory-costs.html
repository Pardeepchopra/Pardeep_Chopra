Inventory Costs

Practical reference covering purchase, ordering, carrying, storage, financing, stock-out and obsolescence costs.

1. Understanding Inventory Cost

Inventory cost is not limited to the purchase price of material. The total economic impact includes the cost of acquiring, ordering, storing, financing, handling, insuring and eventually disposing of inventory.

Effective inventory management therefore requires balancing material availability against the total cost of holding and replenishing stock.

2. Major Categories of Inventory Cost

Cost CategoryWhat It RepresentsManagement Concern
Purchase CostPrice paid to acquire the material.Price, specification, supplier and commercial terms.
Ordering CostAdministrative and transaction effort associated with placing and processing orders.Order frequency and procurement efficiency.
Carrying CostCost associated with keeping inventory over time.Excess stock and capital tied up in inventory.
Storage CostSpace, racks, handling equipment, preservation and warehouse resources.Space utilization and storage efficiency.
Financing CostCapital blocked in inventory.Working-capital impact.
Insurance CostInsurance associated with stored materials where applicable.Risk protection versus cost.
Obsolescence CostLoss when material can no longer be economically used.Ageing and demand review.
Stock-out CostOperational consequence of material unavailability.Production interruption and service risk.

3. Inventory Carrying Cost

Carrying cost is commonly expressed as a percentage of average inventory value per year.

Annual Carrying Cost = Average Inventory Value × Carrying Cost Rate

The carrying-cost rate may incorporate financing/capital cost, storage, insurance, handling, deterioration, obsolescence and other applicable components.

There is no single universal carrying-cost percentage. The organization should establish its methodology and document which cost components are included.

4. Ordering Cost

Ordering cost can include:

  • Purchase requisition processing.
  • Quotation and enquiry administration.
  • Technical and commercial evaluation.
  • Purchase order processing.
  • Vendor communication.
  • Follow-up and expediting.
  • Receiving and invoice-processing effort.

Increasing order frequency can increase administrative effort even when individual order quantities are small.

5. Stock-out Cost

Stock-out cost is often difficult to quantify because the consequence may be operational rather than a direct invoice.

Possible ConsequenceExample
Production interruptionCritical spare unavailable during equipment failure.
Emergency purchaseMaterial purchased at a premium because normal supply was not available.
Expedited freightAir/urgent transport required to restore supply.
Maintenance delayRepair work held because a required component is unavailable.
Service impactCustomer requirement delayed because required material is unavailable.

6. Inventory Cost and EOQ

The classical EOQ model attempts to balance ordering and carrying costs under defined assumptions.

EOQ = √(2DS / H)

D = Annual demand
S = Ordering cost per order
H = Annual holding cost per unit

Actual industrial purchasing may also need to consider MOQ, quantity discounts, supplier capacity, lead time, criticality, transport economics and other constraints.

7. Industrial Cost-Control Framework

  1. Identify high-value inventory.
  2. Measure average inventory value.
  3. Identify excess and slow-moving stock.
  4. Measure ordering frequency.
  5. Review MOQ and supplier constraints.
  6. Analyze stock-out incidents.
  7. Review carrying-cost assumptions.
  8. Use classification techniques to prioritize management attention.

8. Key Management Indicators

Average Inventory ValueValue of inventory maintained over the selected period.
Inventory TurnoverRelationship between annual consumption/cost and average inventory.
Inventory DaysApproximate days represented by average stock.
Carrying CostEstimated annual cost of maintaining inventory.
